Countable nouns can be counted as separate items, while uncountable nouns are usually treated as a mass or substance. This difference affects articles, quantifiers, and verb choices.

---

## Countable nouns

Countable nouns have singular and plural forms.

- *one book / two books*
- *a student / several students*

Singular countable nouns usually need a determiner.

- *I bought **a** chair.*
- *The book is on the table.*

## Uncountable nouns

Uncountable nouns are not normally used with *a/an* and do not usually have a plural form.

- *water*
- *information*
- *advice*
- *furniture*

We usually talk about quantity with expressions such as:

- *some water*
- *a piece of advice*
- *a bit of information*

## Common quantifiers

| Countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- |
| many | much |
| a few / few | a little / little |
| several | a bit of |
| a number of | a large amount of |


Some words work with both types:

- *some*
- *any*
- *a lot of*
- *enough*

## Nouns that change meaning

Some nouns can be countable or uncountable with a change in meaning.

- *chicken* = food, *a chicken* = an animal
- *paper* = material, *a paper* = a newspaper or article
- *experience* = life knowledge, *an experience* = an event

> [!NOTE]
> Many learner errors with articles come from not knowing whether a noun is countable or uncountable in English.

## Common mistakes

- *an advice* -> *some advice* / *a piece of advice*
- *many information* -> *much information* / *a lot of information*
- *I have homeworks* -> *I have homework*
